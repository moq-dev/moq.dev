## Reclaiming the Claim
I'll be honest, I wrote the original blog post about voice AI because I wanted to dump on WebRTC.
I barely understand how voice AI works, but that didn't stop me from barfing my opinions all over the internet.

Hwoever, I'm really proud of one argument I made and wish I expanded upon it:

Our human brains produce and consume audio close to real-time.
We can definitely tolerate some bursts, but it's disorienting.
WebRTC is designed to avoid these bursts, forcing audio to be transmitted/received at a fixed rate and dropping the rest.
It's a better experience for us humans.

But our robot overlords can produce and consume audio expontentially faster.
Throw more sillicon at the problem and it go fast.
But WebRTC prevents robots from going faster; anything sent/received faster than real-time is dropped.

That's the fundamental problem with WebRTC for voice AI.
It's wired for human latency and the human clock rate.

## Speech-to-Speech
But the latest development in voice AI is speech-to-speech.
It's notable because it streams audio in/out closer to real-time.
AKA the human clock rate.

It's a huge step forward for voice AI.
No more separate SST+LLM+TTS passes that add latency and causing an "unnatural" user experience.
I applaud OpenAI for leading the pack on this one.

**DISCLAIMER**: I have zero idea how these models actually work so I'm talking out of my ass (again).

But I don't think you should knee-cap your model by forcing a fixed clock rate.
- Your silicon is capable of waiting for a more complete prompt, then processing faster than real-time.
- Your silicon is capable of generating audio faster rate than real-time, when the user is not speaking ofc.

Don't let the transport layer hold you back bro.
Just because your model supports a tiny buffer doesn't mean it should require it.

## Back to Pipecat
Anyway, let's get back to why you should use MoQ instead of WebRTC:

**Incoming audio (STT) has a configurable maximum latency.**
The transcription will usually be streamed real-time, but can be delayed for up to x seconds (during congestion).

**Outgoing audio (TTS) has a configurable minimum AND maximum latency.**
Generated audio will be transmitted ASAP and buffered locally for up to x seconds (like a Youtube video).
The player will skip audio if it doesn't arrive in y seconds (during congestion).

And of course, interruptions are handled gracefully.
Like if we're playing back 15s of TTS and the user interrupts with a different prompt, it will clear the playback buffer.

**This is a significant improvement over WebRTC**.
You're no longer required to have a ~100ms buffer and drop audio after ~100ms.
MoQ can certainly do that, but it's dynamically configurable per-subscription.
