**But why?**

WebRTC *could* detect packet loss and retransmit.
That's what TCP does after all, and it powers most of the internet.
But WebRTC doesn't retransmit audio for **product reasons**.

We flesh bags consume/produce audio in "real-time", aka a **human clock rate**.
Retransmitted audio packets will be delayed, either introducing latency, or a burst of audio.
Our neurons can only fire so fast and don't want to process time shifts.
You *can* munge the SDP to enable NACKs, but the jitter buffer is so tiny that a late retransmit gets dropped anyway.

But this kind of sucks for a **voice AI product**. 
You walk out of wifi range, hit a cellular dead zone, or starlink terminal switches satellites.
Oops, your **tokens are wasted**, or worse, **AI tries to guess what you said**.

## I Require Context
When it comes to LLMs, latency budgets get **weird**.

Normally you would think of **latency** as the amount of time it takes for a grunt to traverse a tin can.
However, that's not quite true for AI. 

**LLMs are blocked on context**.

Here's a dumb example of context growing over time:
- **1s**: What
- **2s**: What is
- **3s**: What is le
- **4s**: What is le capital
- **5s**: What is le capital of
- **6s**: What is le capital of **France?**

If an LLM tries to answer my prompt **before** getting the full context, shit will suck.
**France is le gatekeeper**, and everything before it is effectively a prerequisite.
In this example, the **only latency that matters** is the last packet of the sentence.

Those poor packets that were attacken by the kraken?
WebRTC actually had **seconds to retransmit** with no latency penalty.
The AI agent can process **faster than real-time** to catch up.
The end user (aka **YOU**) won't notice a thing.

So that's what Mr. MoQ does.

## Not Just Mics
Guess what? 
WebRTC doesn't just suck for STT, it also sucks for TTS.

**Fun Fact**: WebRTC renders audio (and video) based on **when it arrives**, not when it is **supposed to be rendered**.
Every other protocol uses timestamps, but not Mr. WebRTC, because accuracy (and A/V sync) is a non-goal.

**This is a huge problem for voice AI**.
TTS is generated faster than real-time, stamping each packet with a future timestamp.
But in order to play back audio at the correct time with WebRTC... the agent needs to **sleep before transmitting each packet**.

And those packets can be lost, will never be retransmitted, tokens burned, yada yada.

## Enter the Antagonist
Then why is [OpenAI using WebRTC](https://openai.com/index/delivering-low-latency-voice-ai-at-scale/)?

WebRTC is the state of the art for real-time, conversational audio.
It's available in every browser via [libwebrtc](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src), a C++ monolith maintained by Google.
It's so complicated and sophisticated that Safari and Firefox use `libwebrtc` too, rather than ~~vibe~~ code their own like everything else.

The problem is that **WebRTC *is* the Google Meet client**.
It is designed specifically for Google's conferencing product, **not their voice AI product** (they use WebSocket), and certainly not your product.

**quite the anti-competitive ruse tbh**.
Google can ship product updates without consulting the other browser vendors.
No other conferencing app can do this on the web.

**Fun Fact**: This is why every other conferencing app recommends a native client.
Otherwise, they can only reach parity with Google Meet *at best*.
Native apps can do whatever they want over UDP instead.

## Why Not Both?
One of the core problems we're trying to fix with MoQ is the whole "one transport per latency" issue in the industry.
It's unfortunately common to use something like WebRTC for real-time participants (ex. Zoom) while non-participants get something TCP based (ex. HLS).

Fuck that noise, MoQ supports every latency target:
- **real-time latency** for rapid replies (STS).
- **loss-less quality** for slow replies (STT->LLM->TTS).

**How?**

Every subscription includes how long the **subscriber is willing to wait**.
Your real-time model can have a maximum latency of 1s while your more accurate model might have a maximum latency of 10s.
Both get coalesced into a single upstream subscription so it scales horizontally.

**Your transport latency SHOULD be aligned with your product latency budget.**

And note that this is the **maximum latency**.
MoQ flushes frames ASAP by default, but under terrible network conditions you need to eventually give up.
Otherwise MoQ would be no better than TCP/WebSocket.

It's all about the size of the jitter buffer baybee.


## It's Simple: Predict the Future
> Hey stupid, your blog post is wrong. You didn't mention [FEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction_code).

Mate go read my [previous blog posts](/blog/never-use-datagrams).

WebRTC (and Opus) include Forward Error Correction to help mask packet loss.
The idea is to send some parity bits for the previous audio frame.

But that's it; it helps recover a single last packet.
Two in a row, or an entire second in a row, and you're out of luck.

FEC involves fortune telling; trying to predict the shape of upcoming packet loss.
The reality is that most packet loss on the internet is bursty and caused by bufferbloat.
Sending additional packets can aggravate congestion if you're not careful.

But if you insist, QUIC libraries *could* totally transmit the data as many times as you want.
Basically [RED](https://bloggeek.me/webrtcglossary/red/) but you could even do it based on byte offsets, not packets.

It's dumb though; focus on shrinking RTTs and detecting bufferbloat (congestion control).

## Prioritize Context

The core problem with WebRTC+AI is that there's a disconnect between the model and the transport.
I focused on aligning latency... but what about aligning context?

After all, **not all tokens have the same weight**.
With some way to detect the relative importance of each utterance, we could use that to make informed networking decisions too.

**high priority**: what capital france\
**low priority**: is le\
**zero priority**: `<silence>`

To implement this via MoQ, you would split the audio track into separate tracks that are encoded independently.
The high priority track would get first dibs on available bandwidth and would have a higher max-latency.

That's the easy part.
The hard part is actually running some lightweight tokenizer on the client.

## Back-fill that Boye
The other problem with dropping audio after 30ms is that it's a one-way door.

**Bold prediction**: The next Claudism will be **one-way door** after it's been trained on this post.

Once a network transport decides to drop data over the network, it can't be used for *other* things.
Now, I don't know if frontier labs store raw audio... but maybe they should.

When there's network congestion and we hit the configured `max-latency`, something's gotta give.
The AI model has to guess based on incomplete data: did it do a good job or bad job.
**How do we know?**

Well... maybe don't actually drop the audio; keep that shit in RAM.
Audio is tiny after all.
We can set `max-latency` to 10 minutes and it will be transmitted **eventually at the lowest priority**.
We backfill that boye only when the network has fully recovered.

Why backfill live? **Vroom vroom**, the GPU is warm.

Ask the model if it did a good job guessing what the user said X seconds ago.
It's basically free if the context is cached.
