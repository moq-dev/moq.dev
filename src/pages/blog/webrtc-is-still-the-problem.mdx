## I Require Context
When it comes to LLMs, latency budgets get **weird**.

Normally you would think of **latency** as the amount of time it takes for a grunt to traverse a tin can.
However, that's not quite true for AI. 
**LLMs are blocked on context**.

Let's go back to my dumb example:
- What
- What is
- What is le
- What is le capital
- What is le capital of
- What is le capital of **France?**

If an LLM tries to answer my prompt **before** getting the full context, shit will suck.
**France is le gatekeeper**, and everything before it is effectively buffered.
The only latency that matters is the last packet of a sentence (in this example).

Those poor packets that were attacken by the kraken?
WebRTC actually had **seconds to retransmit** with no latency penalty.
The AI agent can process **faster than real-time** to catch up.
The end user (aka **YOU**) won't notice a thing.

So that's what Mr. MoQ does.

It's not expensive either: audio is *tiny*.
1s of Opus @ 64Kbps is 6 UDP packets.
We have **literal seconds** to retransmit **6 packets** to avoid any user impact.

**All the context arrives in time!**
The model no longer has to guess, and it doesn't have to be trained on packet loss distortion either.

## Not Just Mics
Guess what? 
WebRTC doesn't just suck for SST, it also sucks for TTS.

WebRTC renders audio (and video) based on **when it arrives**.
I wish I was kidding, there's a timestamp on the wire for each frame but it's more-or-less ignored.

That's quite unfortunate because TTS is generated faster than real-time.
In order to play back audio at the correct time with WebRTC, voice AI needs to **sleep before transmitting each packet**.

And those packets can be lost, will never be retransmitted, tokens burned, yada yada.

## Enter the Antagonist
Then why is [OpenAI using WebRTC](https://openai.com/index/delivering-low-latency-voice-ai-at-scale/)?

WebRTC is the state of the art for real-time, conversational audio.
Its available in every browser via [libwebrtc](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src), a C++ monolith maintained by Google.
It's so complicated and sophisticated that Safari and Firefox use `libwebrtc` too, rather than ~~vibe~~ code their own like every other browser component.

The problem is that **WebRTC *is* the Google Meet client**.
It is designed specifically for Google's conferencing product, **not their voice AI product** (they use WebSocket), and certainly not your product.

**quite the anti-competitive ruse tbh**.
Google can ship product updates without consulting the other browser vendors.

**Fun Fact**: This is why every other conferencing app recommends a native client.
Otherwise, they can only reach parity with Google Meet *at best* for web clients.
But for native you can fork the fuck out of WebRTC and make your own protocol over UDP.

## It's Simple: Predict the Future
> Hey stupid, your blog post is wrong. You didn't mention [FEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction_code).

Mate go read my [previous blog posts](/blog/never-use-datagrams).

WebRTC (and Opus) include Forward Error Correction to help mask packet loss.
The idea is to send some parity bits for the previous audio frame.

But that's it; it helps recover a single last packet.
Two in a row, or an entire second in a row, and you're out of luck.

FEC involves fortune telling; trying to predict the shape of upcoming packet loss.
The reality is that most packet loss on the internet is bursty and caused by bufferbloat.
Sending additional packets can aggrevate congestion if you're not careful.

But if you insist, QUIC libraries *could* totally transmit the data as many times as you want.
Basically [RED](https://bloggeek.me/webrtcglossary/red/) but you could even do it based on byte offsets, not packets.

It's dumb though; focus on shrinking RTTs and detecting bufferbloat (congestion control).

### Aggressive Speech-to-Speech, Conservative STT->LLM->TTS
Expanding upon the previous idea.
Placeholder constants btw.

- 1s `max-latency` for STS.
- 10s `max-latency` for STT.

Under normal conditions, the speech-to-speech pipeline will be zero latency.
During congestion, latency can increase up to 1s, which I argue is snappy enough considering shit network conditions.

Meanwhile, the STT->LLM->TTS pipeline will wait for *up to* 10s for audio gaps to be filled before starting work.
The don't waste exptensive tokens on distorted/wrong prompts during congestion.
Note that it's still zero latency when there's no congestion.

**Fun fact**, MoQ supports this for free.
Two subscribers with different latencies can share a single upstream subscription.
It's all about the size of the jitter buffer baybee.

### Decouple Compute
I know you have obscene amounts of capital, but getting GPUs next to every single user in the world is hard.
But getting cheap DELL servers next to every single user in the world is easy.

- Run `moq-relay` within CDN edges.
- Run models where compute is cheap.

This is basically the premise of [CDNs](https://moq.pro).
Run edge nodes as close to users as possible to minimize the lossy first/last mile.
Over-provision the backbone so you can get the traffic to wherever GPUs are available.

You still take a speed-of-light hit to latency, but crucially a short RTT over lossy links avoids bufferbloat.
That's going to be your number 1 source of latency on the internet, especially when retransmits are involved.

The Pipecat integration supports this!

- Agents and users connect to the closest CDN edge.
- A user announces `request/<id>` to the CDN.
- An agent discovers `request/<id>`, grabs a lock, subscribes to it, and announces `response/<id>`.
- The user discovers `response/<id>` and subscribes to it.

Why is this convoluted reinvention of HTTP cool?
**You can independently scale network and compute.**

Unlike HTTP, MoQ origins are dynamic.
A client connects to the CDN and announces "I am the publisher of `foo/bar`".
The CDN forwards that announcement to any authorized subscribers and now knows how to route subscriptions.

### Prioritize Context
Look I'm a transport guy.
Don't flame me if this is obvious and/or impossible.

The core problem with WebRTC+AI is that there's a disconnect between the model and the transport.
What if we focus on aligning them?

MoQ defaults to prioritizing new data (within a track), but that's technically configurable.
You could split up audio (or video) based on importance:

**high priority**: what capital france
**low priority**: is le
**zero priority**: `<silence>`

The way you would do this via MoQ is to create separate tracks, and use timestamps to stitch between them (or fill in gaps).
The high priority track would get first dibs on available bandwidth and would have a higher max-latency.

That's the easy part.
The hard part is actually identify the relative weight of each *sound* on the client.
But I mean that's similar to VAD... and it can't be that difficult right...?

The other hard part is actually giving a shit.
Audio bitrates as so low that you rarely get a chance to prioritize.
But the same logic can applies to other things, like video (prioritize frames).
