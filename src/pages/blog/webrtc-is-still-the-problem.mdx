## The Speed of You
We flesh bags consume/produce audio in "real-time", aka a **human clock rate**.
Our neurons can only fire so fast.

For a real-time conferencing product like Google Meet, latency needs to be near zero.
Any delay is super fucking annoying, which is why WebRTC does everything in its power to minimize it.

Blocking on retransmissions will cause delays.
It's the fatal flaw with TCP and why WebRTC is UDP based in the first place.
And worse yet, retransmissions will cause a rapid burst of audio on recovery.

This is a **bad user experience**.
Humans do not want to wait, then process a burst of audio at 10x speed.
WebRTC instead primarily\* relies on **human retransmissions**.

When it comes to LLMs, latency budgets get **_weird_**.

Normally you would think of **latency** as the amount of time it takes for a grunt to traverse a tin can.
However, **LLMs are blocked on context**.

Here's a dumb example of context growing over time:
- **1s**: What
- **2s**: What is
- **3s**: What is le
- **4s**: What is le capital
- **5s**: What is le capital of
- **6s**: What is le capital of **France?**

If an LLM tries to answer my prompt **before** getting the full context, shit will suck.
**France is le gatekeeper**, and everything before it is effectively a prerequisite.
In this example, what matters is the **latency of the last packet**.

Those poor packets that were attacken by the kraken?
WebRTC actually had **seconds to retransmit** with no latency penalty.
The AI agent can process **faster than real-time** to catch up without the end user (aka **YOU**) noticing a thing.

So that's what Mr. MoQ does.
No packet left behind: we keep retransmitting until the AI actually needs the audio.

Guess what? 
WebRTC doesn't just suck for STT, it also sucks for TTS.

**Fun Fact**: WebRTC renders audio (and video) based on **when it arrives**, not when it is **supposed to be rendered**.
Every other protocol uses timestamps, but Mr. WebRTC barely looks at them, because accuracy is a non-goal.
Sure, it'll nudge audio and video into sync by a few milliseconds, but good luck sending the video 500ms after the audio.

**This is a huge problem for voice AI**.
TTS is generated faster than real-time, stamping each packet with a future timestamp.
But in order to play back audio at the correct time with WebRTC... the agent needs to **sleep before transmitting each packet**.

And if there's any packet loss, our TTS is lost, will never be retransmitted, tokens burned, yada yada.

## Random Access Memory
**Welcome back to the future Marty**.
We faster than real-time now.

**RAM** has this amazing property of being stupidly expensive.
And being able to store stuff locally.
You know, like audio that needs to be played over the speakers.

We put a timestamp on each audio frame and use that to determine when to play it out of RAM.
Absurd, I know.

## Enter the Antagonist
Then why is [OpenAI using WebRTC](https://openai.com/index/delivering-low-latency-voice-ai-at-scale/)?

WebRTC is the state of the art for real-time, conversational audio.
It's available in every browser via [libwebrtc](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src), a C++ monolith maintained by Google.
It's so complicated and sophisticated that Safari and Firefox use `libwebrtc` too, rather than ~~vibe~~ code their own like everything else.

The problem is that **WebRTC *is* the Google Meet client**.
It is designed specifically for Google's conferencing product, **not their voice AI product** (they use WebSocket), and certainly not your product.

**quite the anti-competitive ruse tbh**.
Google can ship product updates without consulting the other browser vendors.
No other conferencing app can do this on the web.

**Fun Fact**: This is why every other conferencing app recommends a native client.
Otherwise, they can only reach parity with Google Meet *at best*.
Native apps can do whatever they want over UDP instead.


## Fix It Fix It Fix It
So that's why your hyper deluxe ChatGPT™ voice AI model, after billions invested, has the audacity to ask: 
**can you repeat that?**

I could dump on WebRTC for days, but I want to be positive for a change.
**Here's how to fix it.**

First off, I need to acknowledge what WebRTC *does* have:
- [NetEQ](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/main/modules/audio_coding/neteq/g3doc/index.md) is amazing. It changes audio playback speed based on jitter. But not packet loss.
- Audio retransmissions can now be enabled via SDP munging ([disgusting](https://webrtchacks.com/sdp-anatomy/)). But the NetEQ jitter buffer won't wait for them.
- The [playout-delay](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/main/docs/native-code/rtp-hdrext/playout-delay/README.md) RTP extension is perfect for voice AI. But it's video only.

To fix WebRTC, we need to **petition Google** to:
- Implement [playout-delay](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/main/docs/native-code/rtp-hdrext/playout-delay/README.md) for audio.
- Allow the jitter buffer to grow, both in the past and into the future (for TTS).
- Render audio based on timestamps not vibes.
- IDK some RTP extension to bump the track generation (for interruptions).

And of course, petition the wider WebRTC ecosystem to do the same.

## MoQ do Both
One of the core problems we're trying to fix with MoQ is the whole "one transport per latency" issue in the industry.
It's face-palmingly common to use different protocols based on the latency budget.

For example, an all-hands presentation where the speakers use Zoom, while everybody else watches via HLS and can't participate.

Fuck that noise, MoQ supports multiple latency targets with a single connection. 
In the case of voice AI:
- **real-time latency** for rapid replies (STS).
- **loss-less quality** for slow replies (STT->LLM->TTS).

**How?**
Every MoQ subscription includes how long the **subscriber is willing to wait**.

Your real-time model can have a maximum latency of 500ms while your more accurate model might have a maximum latency of 5s.
Both get coalesced into a **single upstream subscription** which can fanout.

## It's Simple: Predict the Future
One more strawman to beat into the dirt.

> Hey stupid, your blog post is wrong. WebRTC doesn't care about packet loss because it uses [FEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction_code).

M8 go read my previous blog posts: [never use datagrams](/blog/never-use-datagrams) and [forward error correction](/blog/forward-error-correction).
This is like my dumber one hot take.

WebRTC (and Opus) include Forward Error Correction to help mask packet loss.
The idea is to stuff a crappier, lower bitrate copy of the previous audio frame into the next packet.

**But only a single last packet**.
It can't even handle two lost packets in a row, let alone a full network blip.

This is fine for WebRTC because it doesn't give a shit about audio older than 30ms.
But it's not fine for voice AI or really anything on the internet.
**Packet loss due to congestion is bursty, not random.**

A common "fix" is to spread these copies over time, so you can handle X lost packets in a row.
See [Opus 1.5](https://opus-codec.org/demo/opus-1.5/).
All of this extra data has a real cost, and if you don't know what you're doing (and you don't), then you will **aggravate bufferbloat**.

In my humble (and attractive) opinion, FEC is best for huge RTTs, like transmitting to le moon.
In `<current year>`, we instead use CDNs to **shrink the lossy first/last mile** which makes retransmissions **OP**.
Why send more bytes spread over 400ms, instead of retransmitting in 40ms?

But if a WebRTC warrior insists, QUIC libraries can retransmit stream data as many times as they want.
Basically [RED](https://bloggeek.me/webrtcglossary/red/) but based on byte offsets, not packets, and fully transparent to the receiver.
That's a **free idea** btw.

## Decouple Compute
I know you have obscene amounts of capital, but getting GPUs next to every single user in the world is hard.
But getting cheap DELL servers next to every single user in the world is easy.

- Run `moq-relay` within CDN edges.
- Run models where GPUs are available.

Like I said earlier, this is basically the premise of [CDNs](https://moq.pro).
A huge number of edge nodes close to users minimizes the **congested first/last mile**.
Over-provision the backbone so you can get the traffic to the GPUs **without congestion**.

**You can independently scale network and compute.**

## Prioritize Context
I'm a transport and ideas guy, not an AI scientest.
Don't flame me if this is obvious and/or impossible.

The core problem with WebRTC+AI is that there's a disconnect between the model and the transport.
This blog focused on aligning latency... but what about aligning context?

After all, **not all tokens have the same weight**.
If we knew the relative importance of each token, we could use that to make informed networking decisions.

**high priority**: what capital france\
**low priority**: is le\
**zero priority**: `<silence>`

To implement this via MoQ, you would split the audio track into separate tracks that are encoded independently.
The high priority track would get first dibs on available bandwidth, and the subscriber would wait longer for it.

But TBH the hard part is actually giving a shit.
Audio bitrates are so low that you rarely get a chance to prioritize anyway, and it's not worth the hassle of doing a client-side tokenizer.
Maybe it could matter for video tho.

## Back-fill that Boye
The other problem with dropping audio after 30ms is that it's a one-way door.

**Bold prediction**: The next Claudism will be **one-way door** after it's been trained on this post.

Once a network transport decides to drop data over the network, it can't be used for *other* things.
Now, I don't know if frontier labs store raw audio... but maybe they should.

*Eventually* the AI model has to stop waiting and guess based on incomplete data.
Did it do a good job or bad job?
**How can we know?**

Well... maybe don't actually drop the audio; keep that shit in RAM.
Audio is tiny after all.
We can set `max-latency` to 10 minutes and it will be transmitted **eventually at the lowest priority**.
We **backfill that boye** only when the network has fully recovered.

Why backfill via MoQ?
Why not save it as a file and train on it later?
