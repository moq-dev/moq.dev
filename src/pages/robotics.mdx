# Robotics

Robots have a lot of cameras.
They're basically cameras with legs or wheels or pinchers or spinning blades or you get the idea.

Teleoperation is becoming less and less relevant, but *something* still needs to monitor the little guys.
A human, a fleet dashboard, an AI model looking for trouble, a recorder for the post-mortem.
And they're all watching over the worst network you've ever seen.

## Pull, not Push
Existing contribution protocols (ex. WebRTC, SRT, RTSP, RTMP) are push-based.
The robot constantly transmits *everything* to a server, watched or not.
Not great when you've got 9 cameras and StarLink is expensive.

**MoQ is pull-based.**
Each camera is a broadcast, split into tracks like `1080p`, `360p`, `audio` and `telemetry`.
Nothing is transmitted until somebody subscribes to a track.

So by default, an AI model subscribes to the 360p track from every camera.
If it spots a kraken, then it subscribes to the 1080p track from that camera.
Camera 7 doesn't touch the uplink until somebody cares about camera 7.

## Shit Networks
Robots tend to be somewhere with a terrible network: cellular, satellite, mesh radio, or nothing at all.
Your bandwidth will fluctuate, and *something* has to give.

**You decide what gives.**
Every subscription has a priority, and they all cooperate over one QUIC connection.
Controls before audio before telemetry before the kraken cam before the rest of the cameras.
When the link degrades, the least important stuff waits.

And it waits, it doesn't disappear.
Boring protocols like RTP give up on a frame after ~100ms, and it's gone forever.
MoQ sends the newest frames first and keeps the old ones in RAM, up to a TTL.

Each subscriber decides how long it's willing to wait:
- The **operator** waits 100ms, skipping ahead to stay real-time.
- The **AI model** waits a second, because it'd rather see every frame.
- The **recorder** waits 30 seconds, backfilling once the link recovers.

The result is a lossy live feed AND a pristine VOD, from the same stream.

## Bonded Pairs
Near the coast? Use cellular *and* satellite.

QUIC supports path migration, so you can switch from WiFi to cellular to satellite without dropping the connection.
There's also a [multi-path extension](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-quic-multipath/) that uses multiple paths at the same time.
It's [behind a feature flag](https://github.com/moq-dev/moq/blob/main/rs/moq-native/Cargo.toml) if you want to give it a spin.

Or go up a layer and open multiple MoQ connections: P2P via [iroh](https://iroh.computer/), a self-hosted relay, a public CDN.
The robot announces its broadcasts on all of them, and every subscription takes the best available path.
Idle connections cost nothing, because nothing flows until somebody subscribes.

## Fan Out
If 100 viewers subscribe to the same track, [moq-relay](https://doc.moq.dev/bin/relay/) merges them into a single upstream subscription.
The robot serves *at most* one copy of each track, no matter how many people are watching.

Relays connect to each other and form a [cluster](https://doc.moq.dev/bin/relay/cluster/), handling discovery, routing, and failover.
Self-host a relay on the robot or the base station to keep everything on your network.
Then cluster it with a public CDN like [moq.pro](https://moq.pro) or [Cloudflare](/blog/first-cdn) when the whole world wants to watch.

## Not Just Media
There's nothing special about media.
It's just timestamped, delta-encoded data, and so is everything else a robot produces.

- The camera? **A track.**
- The GPS? **A track.**
- The joystick? **A track.**
- The killswitch? **A track**, and every robot in the fleet can subscribe to it.

Stamp it all with the same clock and send it over the same connection, so you can put humpty back together on the other side.
WebRTC botches this: metadata needs a separate connection, and the browser doesn't expose timestamps.

Controls work the same way, just backwards.
The operator publishes a `controls` track, and the robot subscribes to it.
Lockout, handoff, or anarchy is your application's call, not the relay's.
The [MoQ Boy](/blog/moq-boy) demo does exactly this, with a GameBoy standing in for the robot.
